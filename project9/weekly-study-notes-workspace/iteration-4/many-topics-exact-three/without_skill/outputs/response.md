## 1. Demand

**Law of Demand:** When price rises, quantity demanded falls, *ceteris paribus* (all else equal).
→ So the **demand curve slopes downward.**

### Determinants of demand (these SHIFT the curve)
Mnemonic: **"TIPEN"**

| Determinant | Example |
|---|---|
| **T**astes | A trend makes a product popular, so demand shifts right |
| **I**ncome | See normal vs. inferior goods below |
| **P**rices of related goods | Substitutes and complements (see below) |
| **E**xpectations | You expect prices to rise next month, so you buy more now |
| **N**umber of buyers | Population grows, so demand shifts right |

**Normal vs. inferior goods**
- **Normal good:** income ↑ → demand ↑ (e.g., restaurant meals)
- **Inferior good:** income ↑ → demand ↓ (e.g., instant noodles)

**Related goods**
- **Substitutes:** price of A ↑ → demand for B ↑ (coffee ↔ tea)
- **Complements:** price of A ↑ → demand for B ↓ (printers ↔ ink)

---

## 2. Supply

**Law of Supply:** When price rises, quantity supplied rises.
→ The **supply curve slopes upward.**

### Determinants of supply (these SHIFT the curve)
Mnemonic: **"TINE"**
- **T**echnology: better tech → supply ↑
- **I**nput prices: inputs cost more → supply ↓
- **N**umber of sellers: more sellers → supply ↑
- **E**xpectations: about future prices

---

## 3. ⚠️ Movement vs. shift (very important)

| | What causes it | What it's called |
|---|---|---|
| **Movement ALONG the curve** | A change in the good's **own price** | Change in **quantity** demanded or supplied |
| **SHIFT of the curve** | A change in a **determinant** | Change in **demand** or **supply** |

**Exam trap:** "The price of pizza fell, so demand for pizza increased." ❌
Correct version: the price fell, so **quantity demanded** increased (a movement along the curve).

---

## 4. Equilibrium

- **Equilibrium:** where **Qd = Qs**
- The equilibrium price is also called the **market-clearing price**

| Price is... | Result | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| **Above** equilibrium | **Surplus** (Qs > Qd) | Sellers cut prices, so price falls |
| **Below** equilibrium | **Shortage** (Qd > Qs) | Buyers bid prices up, so price rises |

---

## 5. Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

**PED = %ΔQd / %ΔP**

| Value | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| \|PED\| > 1 | **Elastic** | Buyers are very responsive to price |
| \|PED\| < 1 | **Inelastic** | Buyers are not very responsive |
| \|PED\| = 1 | **Unit elastic** | Changes are proportional |

**Inelastic examples:** insulin, gasoline in the short run (necessities with few substitutes).

💡 PED is normally negative because of the law of demand. That's why we compare the **absolute value**.

### Midpoint method
**%Δ = (new − old) / [(new + old) / 2] × 100**

**Worked example:** Price rises from $4 to $6, and Qd falls from 120 to 80.
- %ΔQ = (80 − 120) / 100 × 100 = **−40%**
- %ΔP = (6 − 4) / 5 × 100 = **+40%**
- PED = −40 / 40 = **−1**, so |PED| = 1 → **unit elastic**

*(The midpoint method gives the same answer whether price goes up or down.)*

---

## 6. Price Controls

| | Definition | Example | When it's binding | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Price ceiling** | Legal **maximum** price | Rent control | Set **below** equilibrium | **Shortage** |
| **Price floor** | Legal **minimum** price | Minimum wage | Set **above** equilibrium | **Surplus** |

💡 Memory aid: a **ceiling** is only a problem when it's set *low*, and a **floor** is only a problem when it's set *high*. A non-binding control has no effect.

**Class question:** *Why does a binding price ceiling cause a shortage?*
→ At the capped price, **Qd > Qs**. The low price makes buyers want more and sellers offer less, and the price isn't allowed to rise to close the gap.

---

## 7. Consumer & Producer Surplus

- **Consumer surplus (CS)** = willingness to pay − price paid
  *(on a graph: the area below the demand curve and above the price)*
- **Producer surplus (PS)** = price received − cost
  *(on a graph: the area above the supply curve and below the price)*
- **Total surplus = CS + PS**, which is **maximized at equilibrium**

Price controls move the market away from equilibrium, so they **reduce total surplus**.

## 🧠 Self-Test (answers at the bottom)

1. Incomes rise and demand for bus rides falls. What kind of good are bus rides?
2. The price of coffee rises. What happens to demand for tea? Is that a shift or a movement?
3. A new technology lowers production costs. Which curve shifts, and in which direction?
4. Price goes from $10 to $8 and Qd goes from 50 to 70. Calculate PED with the midpoint method. Is demand elastic or inelastic?
5. The government sets a price floor *below* equilibrium. What happens?
6. Why is total surplus maximized at equilibrium?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. An inferior good.
2. Demand for tea increases. Tea's demand curve shifts right (they're substitutes).
3. The supply curve shifts right.
4. %ΔQ = 20/60 = 33.3%; %ΔP = −2/9 = −22.2%; PED ≈ −1.5. Since |PED| > 1, demand is **elastic**.
5. Nothing. The floor is non-binding, so the market stays at equilibrium.
6. Every trade where willingness to pay is at least the cost happens, and no trade where cost is higher than willingness to pay happens.
